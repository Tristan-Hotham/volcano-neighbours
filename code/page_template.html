In the shadow of the volcano

Naples sits between Vesuvius and Campi Flegrei. Yogyakarta looks up at Merapi, Mexico City at Popocatépetl, Taipei at the Tatun volcanoes. This is every volcano with signs of activity in the last 12,000 years, the number of people who live within 10, 30 and 100 km of each one, and when each last erupted.

The Smithsonian Institution's Global Volcanism Program keeps the world's reference list of volcanoes. Its Holocene list, the volcanoes with evidence of activity in roughly the last 12,000 years, holds {{n_volc|int}} of them, from Antarctica to the Arctic and from the middle of the Pacific to the middle of Africa. For each one it also gives the number of people living within 5, 10, 30 and 100 km. Put those numbers on a map and the Ring of Fire around the Pacific becomes a ring of towns and cities: Java, Luzon, Honshu, the highlands of Mexico and Central America, and, on the far side of the world, the Bay of Naples.

Each circle on the map below is a volcano, sized by the number of people within 30 km (you can switch to 10 or 100 km) and coloured by when it last erupted. Click one to draw its 10, 30 and 100 km rings and see who lives inside them. Click a country for its totals, or search for a volcano or a country.

    02 · How many people?Somewhere between half a billion and a lot more

    Adding up the GVP's figures for every volcano gives {{glob.10.sum|m}} people within 10 km, {{glob.30.sum|m}} within 30 km and {{glob.100.sum|bn}} within 100 km. The last number is far too big, because many people live near more than one volcano and are counted again for each. Naples is the classic case: Vesuvius and Campi Flegrei are {{ves_cf_km|int}} km apart, and most of the city lies within 30 km of both. West Java, with a dozen volcanoes in a cluster, is counted many times over.

    To count each person once, I kept only volcanoes whose circles do not overlap, choosing the most crowded first. Those circles hold different people, so their total is a firm lower bound. The true number lies between the two: at least {{glob.10.floor|m}} people within 10 km of a Holocene volcano (and at most {{glob.10.sum|m}}), at least {{glob.30.floor|m}} within 30 km (at most {{glob.30.sum|m}}), and at least {{glob.100.floor|m0}} within 100 km. Within 10 km the two bounds almost meet; at 100 km the lower bound is the useful one, and studies that count people on a population grid, such as Brown and others (2015), put the number within 100 km at roughly 800 million.

    Most volcanoes are not crowded. {{glob.10.zero|int}} of the {{n_pop|int}} volcanoes with figures ({{n10_zero_pct|pc}}) have nobody at all within 10 km, and the median volcano has {{glob.30.median|int}} people within 30 km. The crowds are concentrated: the 20 most crowded volcanoes hold {{top20_share30|pc}} of the 30 km total, and {{n30_1m|int}} volcanoes have a million or more people within 30 km.

    03 · CountriesIndonesia first, then Mexico, Japan and the Philippines

    Indonesia has {{c.IDN.n|int}} Holocene volcanoes and at least {{c.IDN.floor30|m}} people within 30 km of one, {{idn_share_floor30|pc}} of the world's lower-bound total; within 100 km, at least {{c.IDN.floor100|m0}}, much of the population of Java. Mexico follows with at least {{c.MEX.floor30|m}} within 30 km, then Japan ({{c.JPN.floor30|m}}), the Philippines ({{c.PHL.floor30|m}}) and Italy ({{c.ITA.floor30|m}}), where Naples and Rome both sit beside volcanoes. Taiwan has only {{c.TWN.n|int}} Holocene volcanoes, but one of them, the Tatun Volcanic Group, rises on the northern edge of Taipei.

    Within

    Counting volcanoes gives a different list. The United States has the most, {{c.USA.n|int}}, mostly in Alaska and the Cascades, followed by Japan ({{c.JPN.n|int}}) and Indonesia ({{c.IDN.n|int}}), but few Americans live close to one: at least {{c.USA.floor30|m}} within 30 km. Russia, Chile and Iceland have many volcanoes and few neighbours. Central America is the opposite: El Salvador and Guatemala are small countries built around their volcanoes, with at least {{c.SLV.floor30|m}} and {{c.GTM.floor30|m}} people within 30 km.

    04 · The most crowded volcanoesTaipei, Java, Naples and the Mexican highlands

    By people within 30 km, the most crowded volcano in the list is the Tatun Volcanic Group above Taipei ({{top30.0.p30|m}}), then Michoacán-Guanajuato in Mexico ({{top30.1.p30|m}}), Tangkuban Parahu above Bandung ({{top30.2.p30|m}}), Penanggungan near Surabaya ({{top30.3.p30|m}}) and Merapi above Yogyakarta ({{top30.4.p30|m}}). Vesuvius has {{v.vesuvius.p30|m}} people within 30 km and Campi Flegrei {{v.flegrei.p30|m}}; within 10 km, Campi Flegrei has {{v.flegrei.p10|m}} people living on and around the caldera itself.

    Within

    The ranking depends on the distance. Within 10 km, the top places go to volcanic fields that underlie whole cities: Michoacán-Guanajuato ({{top10.0.p10|m}}), the Tatun group ({{top10.1.p10|m}}), Campi Flegrei ({{top10.2.p10|m}}) and Ilopango beside San Salvador ({{top10.3.p10|m}}). Within 100 km, Java takes over: Gede-Pangrango and Salak, both within sight of Jakarta, each have more than {{top100.1.p100|m0}} people within 100 km, and Hakone and Mount Fuji bring in Tokyo and Yokohama. A note of care: for {{n_flat|int}} volcanoes, most of them large volcanic fields, the GVP gives the same figure at 10 and 30 km (Michoacán-Guanajuato has {{top10.0.p10|m}} at every distance), which suggests the count covers the whole field rather than circles round one point.

    05 · Recent and nearVolcanoes that are both busy and crowded

    A volcano that last erupted 5,000 years ago is not the same threat as one that erupted last year. {{by_ecls.0.n|int}} volcanoes have erupted since 2000, and {{recent_n30_1m|int}} of them have a million or more people within 30 km. Top of that list is Tangkuban Parahu, which last erupted in {{recent.0.last|yr}} with {{recent.0.p30|m}} people within 30 km, then Merapi ({{recent.1.p30|m}}), Slamet ({{recent.2.p30|m}}), Pacaya near Guatemala City ({{recent.3.p30|m}}) and Taal, south of Manila ({{recent.4.p30|m}}). In all, at least {{since1900.floor30|m}} people live within 30 km of a volcano that has erupted since 1900.

And {{by_ecls.5.n|int}} volcanoes have no dated eruption at all: they are on the list because of young lava flows or other Holocene evidence, not because anyone saw them erupt.

    06 · Eruptions through timeMore eruptions, or more people watching?

    The GVP's eruption list holds {{n_erupt|int}} confirmed eruptions since the start of the Holocene. Counted by century, they climb steeply: {{cc.1600.n|int}} in the 1600s, {{cc.1700.n|int}} in the 1700s, {{cc.1800.n|int}} in the 1800s and {{cc.1900.n|int}} in the 1900s. In the {{cc.2000.years|int}} years from 2000 to {{latest_year|yr}} there have already been {{cc.2000.n|int}}, about {{rate_2000|int}} a year against {{rate_1900|int}} a year in the twentieth century.

    This is a record of watching, not of the Earth speeding up. Small eruptions on remote volcanoes go unrecorded unless someone is there to see them or a satellite to detect them, and {{obs_share|pc}} of all eruptions in the list are dated by reported observation. Large eruptions leave deposits that geologists can find and date long afterwards, so their record is more complete. The comparison shows it: from the 1600s to the 1900s the number of recorded eruptions rose {{ratio_all_1900_1600|f1}} times, but the number of eruptions of VEI 4 or more only {{ratio_vei4_1900_1600|f1}} times, and even that rise is likely to be mostly better reporting. {{n_volc_first_after1800|int}} volcanoes have no eruption in the list before 1800.

    07 · The deadliestWhen volcanoes and people met

    Volcano deaths are rare but concentrated. NOAA's Significant Volcanic Eruptions Database lists {{ncei.n|int}} eruptions with deaths recorded, from 141 BCE to {{ncei.y1|yr}}, with {{ncei.deaths|int}} deaths between them. The deadliest are Tambora in 1815 ({{ncei.top.0.deaths|int}} deaths in this database, mostly from the famine that followed), Krakatau in 1883 ({{ncei.top.1.deaths|int}}, mostly from tsunamis), Ilopango in El Salvador in about 450 CE ({{ncei.top.2.deaths|int}}, the date and toll NOAA gives), Mont Pelée in 1902, which destroyed the town of Saint-Pierre on Martinique ({{ncei.top.3.deaths|int}}), and Nevado del Ruiz in 1985, whose mudflows buried the town of Armero ({{ncei.top.4.deaths|int}}).

    08 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Volcanoes and eruptions. The volcano list is the GVP's Holocene list from Volcanoes of the World 5.3.5 ({{n_volc|int}} volcanoes), and the eruption list is its list of confirmed Holocene eruptions from the same version ({{n_erupt|int}} eruptions). Both were downloaded from the GVP in 2026; I used copies posted to public GitHub repositories, whose headers give the version and download date. A volcano's last eruption is the GVP's "last known eruption": for a long eruption that is when it ended, or the present year if it is still going on.

    Populations. Current downloads of the GVP list no longer include population, so the populations within 5, 10, 30 and 100 km come from a copy of the list from version 5.1.7 that still has them, matched by volcano number. {{meta.n_nopop|int}} volcanoes added since then have no figures. The same population figures appear unchanged in a GVP export from 2020, so they are a fixed snapshot of a gridded population estimate, not a census, and they predate a decade of growth in many of these places. The export does not say which population grid or year they come from. For {{n_flat|int}} volcanoes, most of them large volcanic fields, the figure is the same at 10 and 30 km, and I flag those.

    Counting each person once. Summing the figures over volcanoes counts some people several times. For a lower bound I sort volcanoes by population and keep each one whose circle does not overlap a circle already kept; the people inside non-overlapping circles are all different people, so their sum cannot be too high. It can be too low, because it leaves out everyone who lives only near a volcano that was skipped. The truth is between the bounds. Countries use the same method on their own volcanoes; a volcano on a border, such as those between Chile and Argentina, counts for both, and its circles take in people on both sides.

    What this is not. Living within 30 km of a volcano is not the same as being at risk: hazards depend on the volcano, the type of eruption, wind, valleys, rivers and warning systems, and the GVP's list includes volcanoes that may never erupt again. These are distances, not hazard zones.
